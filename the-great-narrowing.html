The pattern we stopped seeing

Crowded teeth. Braces as a rite of passage. Wisdom teeth scheduled for surgical removal like a tax. Long, narrow faces; pinched nostrils; mouth-breathing children; palates too small for their own tongues.

THE NARROWING
Stakes

First: what a mouth is for

The human tooth row is disarmed. Every other ape carries projecting canines — weapons, and advertisements of weapons. Ours stand down into line, a change visible from Ardipithecus onward that Darwin, and paleoanthropologists since, have read as selection against aggression itself.

The smile is built on that disarmament. Primatology traces it to the silent bared-teeth display — a gesture that shows the teeth precisely to signal they will not be used: submission in despotic species, affiliation and benign intent in tolerant ones like bonobos and us; in chimpanzees it measurably reduces aggression and increases affinity. A smile is a disarmament inspection we pass dozens of times a day — and it works because what we show is a smooth, even, benign row.

Your instincts already know this. Give a film or video-game villain one feature — a full mouth of points — and no dialogue is needed; the unease you feel is the old signal running in reverse. Small children make the same read in the wrong direction: they famously misinterpret a dog’s bared-teeth snarl as a smile, a receiver error the dog-bite-prevention literature works hard to train out of them.

So hold this for the rest of the deck: the mouth is a social organ before it is a mill. What follows is the story of how we began building it badly.

Sources: van Hooff (1972) · Preuschoft & van Hooff (1997) · Waller & Dunbar, Ethology 111:129 (2005) · Suwa, Lovejoy et al., Science 326 (2009) · Cieri et al., Curr. Anthropol. 55:419 (2014) · Meints et al., dog-bite-prevention studies
THE NARROWING
The archaeological baseline

The skulls say it wasn’t always so

Pre-industrial skulls — from any continent — overwhelmingly show broad dental arches, straight teeth, and erupted, functional third molars. Decay was so rare that archaeologists treated a carious tooth as evidence a skull wasn’t ancient (Dryer, Nature, 1935).

Price examined 1,276 skulls of ancient Peru: not one showed the deformity pattern now routine in modern mouths. In Swiss ossuaries — Naters alone holds some 20,000 skeletons — teeth centuries old sat sound in their sockets, while the recently buried lay riddled with decay.

A 2007 study compared medieval Norwegian skulls with living Norwegians: the medieval dead had significantly better occlusion than their modern descendants.

Sources: Price (1939), chs. 2, 13 · Dryer, Nature 136:302 (1935) · Evensen & Øgaard, Am. J. Orthod. Dentofacial Orthop. 131:710 (2007)
THE NARROWING
Newburgh, Ontario → Cleveland, Ohio

The man who went looking

Weston Andrew Price — born on a farm near Newburgh, Ontario, 1870; D.D.S. from Michigan at 23; a Cleveland practice with a chemistry laboratory upstairs analyzing food samples mailed in from around the world. By 1915 he had published over 150 research papers.

That year he became founding director of dentistry’s first great research arm — the Research Institute of the National Dental Association, the body that became the American Dental Association — and chaired the profession’s research section from 1914 to 1928, directing a staff of sixty with an advisory board that included Charles Mayo and the sitting president of the AMA. He stood at the summit of organized dentistry during the very years it taught the world to brush.

Which sharpens what came next. Convinced the answer to degeneration would not be found in diseased tissue but in whatever the healthy still possessed, he and his wife Florence spent the 1930s traveling to the last places untouched by modern commerce — photographing, charting, and chemically analyzing the people there before the roads and store food arrived. The immune groups he found had never seen a toothbrush. His conclusion was not that hygiene is worthless — it was that hygiene was never the lever.

Study design, 1931–1939

The method: living controls

Same people, same genes, different food. In every region he sought two groups of the same stock: one isolated on its traditional diet, one at the point of contact with imported white flour, sugar, and canned goods. Each people served as its own control.

The pattern repeated at every latitude, every altitude, and in every ancestry: the isolated groups thrived; the modernized — and above all their children — deteriorated on cue. Whatever was doing this did not care about race. It tracked the food.

Across all fourteen regions, susceptibility to decay rose an average of 35-fold with the change of food — while tooth-brushing was essentially absent in the immune groups. As Harvard’s Earnest Hooton put it in 1937: “It is store food which has given us store teeth.”

THE NARROWING
Finding two

The face itself changes

Decay was the least of it. In children born after their parents took up the displacing foods, the facial skeleton came in different: narrowed dental arches, crowded teeth, pinched nostrils, an underdeveloped middle third of the face, mouth breathing. Not damage to formed bone — a change in how the bone formed.

Source: Price (1939), ch. 3, Figs. 3–4
THE NARROWING
The decisive evidence

The control no lab could build: families

Fathers with broad faces — and their own sons, born after the diet changed, without them. Older siblings normal; the younger, conceived after modernization, narrowed — every time in the same direction. Same parents, same household, unmixed ancestry by Price’s explicit verification. Whatever did this arrived between one pregnancy and the next, and it arrived in the food.

Source: Price (1939), ch. 17, Figs. 98–108
THE NARROWING
His theory

Price’s explanation: intercepted heredity

His conclusion was more radical than “eat your vitamins.” The injury, he argued, is set before birth: displacing foods deplete the parents — both parents — of minerals and the fat-soluble activators (vitamins A and D, and a third factor he called Activator X), degrading the germ cells and the gestational environment. The child’s blueprint is intact; its expression is intercepted.

  • Hale’s pigs (Texas, 1930s): sows deprived of vitamin A before mating farrowed 59 piglets, in six litters, born without eyeballs; a single dose of cod-liver oil before mating produced partial eyes. Parental deficiency alone deforms offspring structure.
  • Birth order: the later children of depleting mothers fare worst — his sibling photographs in one line.
  • Tribal practice: peoples worldwide fed special foods to both prospective parents; in some groups, six months of special feeding was required before marriage.
  • Even third molars: he attributed impaction and failure-to-form to the vitamin A status of the mother in gestation and of both parents before conception.
  • Soil: depleted fields grow depleted food, which builds depleted parents — his chapter 20.
Sources: Price (1939), introduction & chs. 17–18, 20 · Hale, J. Heredity 24:105 (1933) & Texas Agr. Exp. Station reports
THE NARROWING
Anticipated rebuttals, 1939

He saw the objections coming

What they said then

  • “It’s race mixing.”
  • “It’s heredity — one parent’s big teeth, the other’s small jaw.”
  • “Thumb sucking. Mouth breathing. Sleeping habits.”
  • “It’s just poverty.”

His answer

  • Appears in unmixed groups — in the first generation after the food changes.
  • The same two parents produce both normal and narrowed children — ordered by the date the household diet changed.
  • The deformity precedes the habits: visible in infancy; mouth breathing follows the narrowed airway, it doesn’t cause it.
  • It struck the prosperous modernized and spared the poorest isolated.

One objection he could not answer in 1939: no controlled experiment separated his variables. The food that lost its minerals had also gone soft. That test came later — largely from his critics’ side of the field.

Sources: Price (1939), chs. 1–2, 11, 17
II

The verdict of later science

Everything checkable was eventually checked — mostly by people who never read him. Here is how it came out.

THE NARROWING
Audit before advocacy

First, the honest ledger

What doesn’t hold up

  • Observational fieldwork: no blinding, no randomization; photographs can be selected.
  • “Injured germ plasm” sounded Lamarckian to mid-century genetics — and was dismissed with the rest.
  • The era’s vocabulary — “primitive races,” “stocks” — aged terribly and made him unquotable.
  • Activator X went unidentified in his lifetime; nutrition read as his single cause for everything.

What survives audit

  • The core pattern: skeletal & dental deterioration within one generation of dietary modernization, independent of ancestry.
  • The prenatal timing of the injury.
  • The vitamin-A mechanism, demonstrated in animals.
  • The soil chapter.

Every item in the right-hand column was later confirmed — by researchers who, for the most part, never cited him. The next six slides are that record.

 
THE NARROWING
Vindication I · anthropology, 1984

The occlusal transition

In 1984 — Price long dead and long unfashionable — anthropologist Robert Corruccini published, in the flagship journal of orthodontics, evidence of an “epidemiologic transition” in dental occlusion: malocclusion rises with urbanization, at the speed of urbanization. That signature points to environment, not genes.

Rural Kentucky. Punjab. Bengal. The Pima of Arizona. In each: traditionally-raised elders with sound occlusion — and their own children, raised on soft modern food, without it. Among dietary variables, food toughness was the single best predictor.

Rediscovered. Measured. Mainstream. And structurally identical to Price’s field pattern of fifty years before.

1–2
generations: typical time for a population’s occlusion to go from good to poor after modernization
Sources: Corruccini, Am. J. Orthodontics 86:419 (1984) · Corruccini, How Anthropology Informs the Orthodontic Diagnosis of Malocclusion’s Causes (1999)
THE NARROWING
Vindication II · the lab

The controlled experiments

The mechanical lever · confirmed

Animals fed identical nutrients in soft versus hard form develop malocclusion on the soft feed — shown in primates, rodents, hyraxes, minipigs. Chewing load during growth is a real, isolable cause of arch narrowing. This is the experiment Price never ran, performed by the researchers who corrected him.

The nutritional lever · confirmed

Meanwhile his mechanism entered the veterinary textbooks: maternal vitamin A deficiency causes congenital defects in pigs and cattle — abnormal development of the cranial bones, hydrocephalus, blindness. Severe parental deficiency deforms the fetal skull. Hale’s pigs were no fluke.

Two levers. Both environmental. Both real. Price bundled them; later science unbundled them — and nothing in the unbundling rescued the theory both had replaced: that it was “just heredity.”

Sources: Corruccini & colleagues, soft-diet animal models (1980s–90s) · Ciochon et al., minipig masticatory study (1997) · Lieberman et al., J. Hum. Evol. 46:655 (2004) · standard veterinary pathology references on hypovitaminosis A
THE NARROWING
Vindication III · the archaeological record

The whole skeleton, not just the jaw

−50–75%

Trabecular — spongy — bone density of recent humans, versus chimpanzees. A 2015 PNAS study CT-scanned limb joints across millions of years of the human line: density stayed high through every fossil hominin, including early Homo sapiens — and collapsed only in recent, sedentary Holocene humans.

Foragers held it. Farmers lost some. We lost more. The primary driver is mechanical loading — the moving, carrying, chewing body — with nutrition setting the material budget.

“Loss of proper bone structure and mass” is no longer a claim. It is a measured fact of the skeletal record.

Sources: Chirchir et al., PNAS 112:366 (2015) · Ryan & Shaw, PNAS 112:372 (2015) · Saers et al., J. Hum. Evol. (2016) · Evensen & Øgaard (2007)

Populations still chewing traditional food grow jaws their teeth fit; their urban cousins do not — within a generation. And a note of caution for casual observation: agenesis runs highest in East Asia (~30%) and lower in Africa — so a full set of erupted third molars marks a jaw grown to size, not a genetic gift of any ancestry.

Price’s reading (parental vitamin A governs whether the tooth forms and fits) and the modern reading (arch under-growth plus tooth-number genetics) likely share the truth. Either way: impaction is a mismatch we manufacture, then bill for.

THE NARROWING
Synthesis

Three levers on one skeleton

1 · The foundation

Preconception & prenatal nutrition — both parents. Sets the congenital pattern before the child eats a meal. Price’s core claim; today’s “developmental programming.”

2 · The material

Minerals & fat-soluble vitamins through growth. Build enamel, mineral density, growth-plate quality, caries resistance. Price’s laboratory chemistry.

3 · The load

Chewing, breastfeeding mechanics, carrying, walking. Tell the arch how wide — and the bone how dense — to build. The lever Price missed; his critics’ lasting contribution.

All three are environmental. Which means all three are choices — made for most of us, mostly, before we could chew.

Synthesis of: Price (1939) · Corruccini (1984–99) · Lieberman (2004, 2013) · Chirchir / Ryan & Shaw (2015) · developmental-programming literature (2000s– )
THE NARROWING
The cleanest proof

The immigrant key

Meet an adult raised in a village on tough, mineral-dense food: broad arch, third molars in working service. Their children — born in Toronto or Chicago into the soft industrial diet — arrive in orthodontists’ chairs like everyone else’s.

Nothing “racial” is being preserved or lost. A developmental environment is. Price photographed exactly this in every population he visited; Corruccini measured it in the children of migrants and the children of modernizing villages alike. It is the single cleanest demonstration that the modern face is made, not inherited — and it cuts both ways: no ancestry is safe, and none is doomed.

Every generation gets a fresh vote.

Sources: Price (1939), chs. 3–14, 17 · Corruccini, Am. J. Orthodontics (1984)
III

The receipts

Wherever a skeleton carries a price tag, none of this was ever controversial.

THE NARROWING
Exhibit A · 1932–1942

Pottenger’s cats

Francis Pottenger Jr. fed roughly 900 cats identical rations, raw or cooked. The cooked-meat lines slid generation by generation: stillbirths, low birth weight, fragile and deformed skeletons, failing reproduction. His framing — “raw food” — was wrong, and his later fans took it strange places. His inference was exact: some unknown, heat-labile factor was being destroyed in the cooking.

1973–90
feline research identifies & characterizes the factor: taurine, degraded by heat
400 / 500
mg per kg diet — NRC taurine requirement today: cats / pregnant queens

Every deficiency sign he recorded now maps, condition for condition, onto documented taurine deficiency. Observation validated. Mechanism renamed. Originator forgotten. Remember the shape of that story — you are watching it happen to Price.

Sources: Davidson & Emlet, Logan Univ., “Pottenger’s Cats Revisited” (Exp. Biol. 820.4, 2014) · Pottenger, Am. J. Orthod. Oral Surg. 32:467 (1946) · NRC, Nutrient Requirements of Cats (1986)
THE NARROWING
Exhibit B · the cattle literature

“Fetal programming” — his thesis, industrialized

Ask why a calf grew wrong, and no rancher says “genes” and shrugs. An entire modern field — developmental programming — documents how the dam’s nutrition before and around conception and through gestation sets the calf’s muscle, bone, organs, and lifetime performance, working partly through epigenetic regulation of the fetal genome. University extension services teach it to producers as routine herd management.

Read the field’s founding definition beside Price’s “intercepted heredity” and try to find the daylight. There isn’t any. There is only a sixty-year gap and a missing citation.

Hale’s pigs. Price’s chapter 18. Standard veterinary medicine — for animals with owners.

THE NARROWING
Exhibit D · where bone is the asset

Million-dollar skeletons

Horses

Developmental orthopedic disease in foals triggered copper epidemiology across Ohio and Kentucky stud country, revisions to NRC copper recommendations, and supplementation trials in pregnant mares. Copper-depleted lambs develop osteoporosis; copper-deficient farmed deer, osteochondrosis. When the skeleton is the asset, trace minerals get budget lines — and honest complications (copper wasn’t the sole cause) get follow-up studies instead of dismissal.

Hens

About 30% of caged laying hens suffer at least one fracture in their lives — so poultry science runs a permanent research program on bone breaking strength: calcium level and particle size, phosphorus, vitamins D and K, exercise, genetics. Bone quality engineered to the gram, for a four-dollar bird.

The question this presentation asks is not whether skeletal quality can be built by nutrition and load. That question is settled — wherever it pays.

Sources: Pearce et al., Equine Vet. J. 30:211 (1998) · Suttle et al., J. Comp. Path. 82:93 (1972) · Thompson et al. (1994) · Fleming, Proc. Nutr. Soc. 67:177 (2008) · Whitehead, Poult. Sci. (2004)

The soil map

Each tag below is a regional disease of livestock — named by the farmers who watched their animals waste — that turned out to be the soil itself, deficient in cobalt, copper, or selenium:

New Zealand’s pumice country was nearly impossible for sheep and cattle until cobalt topdressing of the pasture; white muscle disease vanished from Canterbury lambs with selenium in the 1950s. Depleted soil → depleted feed → depleted creatures. Price’s least respectable chapter became a branch of agronomy.

IV

What it means

The knowledge was never lost. Look where it was applied.

THE NARROWING
The uncomfortable ledger

The double standard

The herd

  • Preconception nutrient targets for dams and sires.
  • Gestation-specific vitamin & mineral requirements, revised continuously for 80 years.
  • Soil tested, mapped, and amended.
  • A deformed animal treated as a management failure with a cause.

The public

  • Prenatal advice compressed to folic acid and “a balanced diet.”
  • Dietary guidance contested by industry and ideology for a century.
  • Soil quality invisible at the point of sale.
  • Crooked teeth, collapsed arches & fragile bones filed under genetics and bad luck — then billed: braces, extractions, implants, hip pins.

This is the precise sense in which people were shortchanged. Not by a conspiracy — by an asymmetry. Where an under-built skeleton costs its owner money, the science was adopted ruthlessly. Where it costs the person themself — decades later, dispersed, deniable — it stayed on the shelf. Ask a rancher whether a cow’s diet before conception shapes her calf’s skeleton. He won’t recognize it as a question.

Compare: the NRC/NASEM livestock requirement series (1944–present) with the documented history of human dietary guidelines
THE NARROWING
The orphaning

Why you’ve never heard of him

No suppression required — something quieter, and in a way worse. Start with the asymmetry of effort: his dataset cost a decade, fourteen regions, and thousands of plates; the dismissals cost a paragraph. “It’s heredity” was asserted as the default, never demonstrated — a point Corruccini later leveled at his own field’s genetic paradigm. Then Price’s earlier focal-infection research collapsed under 1940s reappraisal — a reviewer declared nearly all pre-1936 pulpless-tooth studies invalid — and everything carrying his name was tarred with it. His 1930s vocabulary became unquotable. The foundation bearing his name harnessed the data to raw-milk crusades that made academics allergic to the citation. And the core findings were re-derived under new names — “epidemiologic occlusal transition,” “evolutionary mismatch,” “developmental programming” — with no trail leading back.

The record was not erased. It was orphaned. “Vindicated but uncredited” is a real fate in science — and from the outside it looks exactly like having been wrong.

That distinction matters for how this story should be told. A suppression narrative invites easy dismissal and can’t be documented; the orphaning can be documented on every slide you have just seen. And note who never needed convincing: the feed industry ran the numbers on its own animals, adopted the framework wholesale — and never once cited the debate.

Cf.: the 1940 reappraisal of pre-1936 pulpless-tooth research · Corruccini (1999) on the heredity assumption in orthodontics
THE NARROWING
Application

What the evidence actually supports doing

Rebuild the foundation

Nutrient-dense food for both prospective parents, before conception and through pregnancy: minerals and the fat-soluble vitamins, from real food. The one window Price called non-negotiable — and the one the cattle literature now guards to the IU.

Supply the material

Children built from mineral-rich whole foods rather than displacement calories. The traditional diets Price analyzed carried several-fold the mineral and fat-soluble-vitamin content of the American diet of his day.

Apply the load

Breastfeeding’s oral workout where possible; food that requires actual chewing throughout childhood; load-bearing movement for life. The skeleton keeps what it is asked to carry.

General education drawn from the historical and scientific record — individual medical, dental, and prenatal decisions belong with your own practitioners.

Sources: Price (1939), chs. 15, 21 · Corruccini (1999) · developmental-programming & bone-loading literature as previously cited
THE NARROWING
Epistemic hygiene

Open questions — say them before skeptics do

  • Activator X: plausibly vitamin K2 (argued in detail in 2007) — plausible, not proven.
  • Adult reversibility: “orthotropics” and “mewing” outrun their evidence. Childhood is where the developmental leverage is; adult claims should be made cautiously or not at all.
  • The mix: how much foundation vs. material vs. load in any given face is unresolved. All three are real; their proportions are not settled.
  • Human paternal effects: early but growing — the sperm-epigenome literature is slowly giving Price’s most-mocked claim, “germ-cell injury,” a modern research program.
  • The un-run experiment: no controlled study has ever morphed tooth sharpness on a human face and measured perceived threat, warmth, or trust. Dentistry’s working doctrine — pointed canines read aggressive, rounded read gentle — has been designed into smiles since the 1950s (“dentogenics”) on clinical intuition. The nearest evidence is indirect: people dislike sharp contours generally, and angry faces read as more threatening with teeth exposed. The direct test is cheap, obvious, and waiting for someone to run it.
  • Measured vs. concluded: hold his photographs and his numbers firmly; hold his moralizing — the “character and degeneracy” passages — loosely. They are of his era, and they are not needed.

A case this strong doesn’t need overstating. Overstating it is how it got orphaned the first time.

Sources: Masterjohn, “On the Trail of the Elusive X-Factor” (2007) · paternal preconception / sperm-epigenetics literature (2010s– ) · Frush & Fisher, J. Prosthet. Dent. 8:558 (1958) · Bar & Neta, Psychol. Sci. 17:645 (2006)
THE NARROWING
Close

The blueprint is intact

What was lost in one generation was never lost in the germ line. Price’s most hopeful finding — the one his own word “degeneration” hides — is that the deformity is not hereditary doom. He wrote that the outlook is poor for the affected individual, but not for that individual’s descendants. Restore the conditions, and the next generation is built to the old pattern.

The narrowing is an environment.
Environments can be changed.
